I recently read this quote from Albert Camus. “A man’s work is nothing but this slow trek to rediscover, through the detours of art, those great and simple images in whose presence his heart first opened.” 

I thought of three times when I felt strongly about such “heart-opening” moments, and all my research is a rediscovery of them. 

The first time is in a contemporary sociology theory class when I first read Pierre Bourdieu. It was my first year studying in the US as a journalism and communication graduate student with no sociology background. I had never taken any sociology class before. I read Bourdieu for the first time, and it completely changed my perspective on society as a system. The first time I read Distinction: A Social Critique of the Judgment of Taste, I was completely captivated by how elegantly he articulated, through tons of examples about schools, language, and leisure to explain how individual taste is formed by social classes and, in turn, reinforces the social structure. It shows me what it means to be a theorist to provide a holistic explanation. It also answers the questions I’ve been bothered by since childhood, such as “what it means to be cool” and “who decides it’s cool”. Later on in that class, we also read Foucault and Latour, which opened the door to the rich, meaningful discussion of cultural sociology for me and left me regret that I wasn’t a sociologist. Yet years later I made many great friends in sociology and always claim that I am a sociologist when there are no real sociologists around. 

The second time is in a class on physics of information and computation, I learned dynamical systems and was stunned by the complex crazy beautiful trajectories of the Lorenz system that are produced by three short lines of linear equations. I didn’t sleep that night and got lost in the Strogatz book on nonlinear systems. All I was thinking was that I’ve seen so many crazy beautiful things in life: raindrops reflect from the turquoise tiles, mushrooms grow out of dying trees, oceans connect to the sky, sunset sinks into darkness, but I’ve also missed this whole world of abstract beauty that I wouldn’t be able to understand intuitively or even imagine. I would have missed all this if I had never learned about dynamical systems and physics of computing. That’s the moment I understood why my advisor Martin insisted that I took two physics classes at the beginning of my Communication PhD. I spent a lot of time learning complex systems and information theory and kept thinking about attractors, phase transitions, state, and bifurcations until now. Yet I still didn’t get a chance to actually use it in my research. 

All of the things I thought of doing in social science, physicists already did them in all kinds of smart ways. What can I do now? I’m not a physicist or sociologist. I can’t contribute anything new or, at least, anything of good quality to the field I was attracted to. I felt the gravity of path dependency of my own research trajectory.

That’s the time my heart opened for the third time, in a class on cultural evolution. It was a coincidence. I accidentally took the evolutionary modelling class in anthropology with Cristina Moya, because the economics department and political science don’t offer Intro to modelling that semester. I fell in love completely with cultural evolution and evolutionary modelling during that seminar. Rather than a moment of shock or revelation, it is the whole process of finding the ultimate explanation for human behaviour. Why do we have the complex and diverse culture today? Why do we differ so much in some ways yet exactly the same in other ways? Why does some information spread so fast among us yet some never get around? Why are we so ashamed of some of the things even when there are no “fitness consequences”? I spent hours discussing with my professor and friends those topics and inventing the most impossible explanations and proving them or disproving them with toy models. 

Aside from having a lot of fun with those toy models, I surprisingly found that cultural evolution provides a great framework to connect my interest in cultural sociology and in complex systems. More specifically, it provides me a way of thinking about iteration, inheritance, emergence, and maintenance of a system. I love how subtle and how few assumptions cultural evolutionary models usually are, and that cultivates a sense of aesthetics of modelling in me. Most importantly, the most valuable gifts cultural evolution has bestowed on me are the connections I have made with the most interesting, motivated, and complex people in the world. 

Those are the moments that ground me in the scientific path I am on now and will continue to pursue with deep passion for cultural sociology, complexity science, and cultural evolution.
