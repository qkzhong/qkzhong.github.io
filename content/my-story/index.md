I recently read this quote from Albert Camus. “A man’s work is nothing but this slow trek to rediscover, through the detours of art, those great and simple images in whose presence his heart first opened.”

There have been three times when I felt strongly about such “heart-opening” moments, and all my research is a rediscovery of them.

The first time is in a contemporary sociology theory class when I first read Pierre Bourdieu. It was my first year studying in the US as a journalism and communication student with no sociology background. Reading Bourdieu changed my entire perspective on society as a system. When I read Distinction: A Social Critique of the Judgment of Taste, I was captivated by how elegantly he articulated, through tons of examples about schools, language, and leisure, how individual taste is formed by social classes and, in turn, reinforces the social structure. It shows me what it means to be a theorist to provide a holistic explanation. It also answers the questions I’ve been bothered by since childhood, such as “what it means to be cool” and “who decides it’s cool”. Later on in that class, we also read Foucault and Latour, which opened the door to the rich, meaningful discussion of cultural sociology for me and left me regret that I wasn’t a sociologist. Years later, I made many great friends in sociology and always claim to be a sociologist when there are no real sociologists around.

The second time is in a class on physics of information and computation, I learned dynamical systems and was stunned by the complex crazy beautiful trajectories of the Lorenz system that are produced by three short lines of linear equations. I didn’t sleep that night and got lost in the Strogatz book on nonlinear systems. All I was thinking was that I’ve seen so many crazy beautiful things in life: raindrops reflect from the turquoise tiles, mushrooms grow out of dying trees, oceans connect to the sky, sunset sinks into darkness, but I’ve also missed this whole world of abstract beauty that I wouldn’t be able to understand intuitively or even imagine. I would have missed all this if I had never learned about dynamical systems and physics of computing. That’s the moment I understood why my advisor Martin insisted that I take two physics classes at the beginning of a social science PhD. I spent a lot of time learning complex systems and information theory and kept thinking about attractors, phase transitions, state, and bifurcations until now. Yet I still didn’t get a chance to actually use it in my research.

All of the things I thought of doing in social science, physicists already did them in all kinds of smart ways. What can I do now? I’m not a physicist or a sociologist. I couldn't contribute anything new or at least anything of good quality that I expected of myself. I felt the gravity of path dependency of my own research trajectory.

That’s the time my heart opened for the third time, in a class on cultural evolution. It was a coincidence. I accidentally took the evolutionary modelling class in anthropology with Cristina Moya, because the economics and political science didn’t offer the intro-level modelling class that semester. Naturally, I fell in love with cultural evolution and evolutionary modelling during that seminar. Rather than a moment of revelation, it is the whole process of finding the ultimate explanation for human behaviour that made me realize that this is the path I want to take. Why do we have the complex and diverse culture today? Why do we differ so much in some ways yet exactly the same in other ways? Why does some information spread so fast among us yet some never get around? Why are we so ashamed of some of the things even when there are no “fitness consequences”? I spent hours discussing with my professor and friends those topics and inventing the most impossible explanations and proving them or disproving them with toy models.

This is also the time I surprisingly found that cultural evolution provides a great framework to connect cultural sociology and complex systems holistically. Specifically, it provides me a way of thinking about iteration, inheritance, emergence, and maintenance of a system. I love how subtle evolutionary models are and how few assumptions they rely on. This cultivates a sense of modeling aesthetics in me. More importantly, cultural evolution connects me to the most interesting, motivated, and complex people in the world. It is now really their fault that there are no other paths in science left for me.

So now I will continue my path and keep writing this love story for culture and evolution.
